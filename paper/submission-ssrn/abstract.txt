Under CECL a lender may estimate its allowance for credit losses with any reasonable method, and the SEC expects it to document an identified range of expected losses. This paper measures how much of that range comes from the choice among equally accurate default models. On 49,406,273 Freddie Mac mortgages, at each of 21 year-ends from 2005 to 2025, I train 65 models on what a lender knew at the time, keep those within one bootstrap standard error of the best held-out log loss, and compute the lifetime allowance of the same portfolio under each, with the prepayment model, the economic forecast, the reversion policy and loss severity held fixed. The design and six predictions were recorded before the data were opened. Across these models the allowance ranges over a median of 55% of the most accurate model's (17% and 6% among the few models that a paired test at two or one standard errors cannot separate from the best), up to 18.0 billion dollars on Freddie Mac's active book, and more than the gap between one- and three-year reversion at every date. In comparisons added after the results, refitting the best model on independent samples of the same size moves the allowance at least as far as switching among twenty equally accurate models (61% against 32% where the samples are disjoint) and changes more loans' IFRS 9 stage, contrary to the interpretation of a staging prediction that holds as recorded. Realized losses fall inside the range at one of 13 dates: model uncertainty is not forecast uncertainty. The spread was not unusually large in relative terms before the crisis, and monotone constraints barely narrow it. Code and every aggregate result are released.
